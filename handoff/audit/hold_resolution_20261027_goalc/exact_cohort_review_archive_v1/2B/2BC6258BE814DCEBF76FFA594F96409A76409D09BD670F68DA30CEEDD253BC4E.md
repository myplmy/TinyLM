Following this helium-burning phase, the fusion reaction controls the carbon-burning phases in stars during late stellar evolution. But the fusion reaction is also highly important because it triggers the ignition of the supernova explosions that occur in accreting white dwarf systems . This reaction is also thought  to be responsible for the recently observed superbursts, which are sudden bursts of x-ray activity in accreting neutron star systems that last for much longer time periods than the regular ten second x-ray bursts . However, for the presently accepted reaction rates [28, 41, 42] the temperatures in the neutron star crust are too low to trigger the ignition of the fusion process at the expected depth in the star .
Several decades ago, experimental study of the fusion reaction was a major goal, propelled by the discovery of a pronounced resonance configuration that was interpreted as molecular cluster formation . Later measurements [47, 48] focused more on the low-energy contributions and confirmed that the lower energy range is characterized by pronounced resonance structures as well. The reaction rate depends sensitively on a reliable extrapolation of the low-energy data to the Gamow range. While the tabulated rate  relies on an empirical extrapolation of the data , recent attempts use more sophisticated nuclear model techniques . Of particular interest is the suggestion of a “hindrance factor” in the reaction . This would reduce the sub-barrier fusion cross section substantially, which would lead to significant implications for stellar carbon-burning environments . It is clearly of great importance to study the reaction at low energies to provide a more reliable experiment-based reaction rate.
